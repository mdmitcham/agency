A training firm owner told me their biggest client scaled back this year.

One client.

That one change cost them about £150,000 a year.

They didn't get fired. Nothing went wrong with the work. The engagement just got smaller, and there was no second client waiting behind it.

That's what "referrals dried up" often means. A lot of the time it isn't a slow fade. It's one big relationship changing and the business finding out it had no other way in.

## You can't turn referrals up

I hear the same thing on a lot of calls with B2B firms that grew on word of mouth. Different words, same problem.

A franchise consultant: "It's ebbs and flows and I don't like the ebbs."

An operations consultant who sells multi-month engagements: "I just suspect the bigger engagements are going to be far and fewer between."

The president of a leadership development firm: "I don't think that we're gonna get to where we know we could go one lead at a time."

An executive search firm owner with 30 years in the business called referrals "the 90% solution to my problem." Then, a minute later:

"I'm not the guy driving. I have to convince them to talk about me when I'm not in the room."

Same owner, on why the last few years have been slow: "my business is slowed down partly as my network is aging."

Referrals feel like a strategy because they've worked for years. They're really a side effect. Somebody happy happens to know somebody who happens to need you, this month.

You don't control any part of that sentence. And the people who used to send you work get a year closer to retirement every year.

I wrote about this for wealth managers [here](/blog/wealth-managers-paid-traffic-aum/), and Michael Kitces said it best: "the well just goes dry and they referred all the people they know from their personal network."

Nothing's wrong with your clients. They already sent you everyone they know. 🤷

## What most firms try next

When the referrals slow down, most B2B owners reach for one of four things. I've heard how all four go.

Cold email. That leadership firm president: "I could send out 30 messages a day and get one response a week." And the people they did reach "won't even read the emails because they think it's a bot... I've even written in there, I physically am typing this myself."

LinkedIn. An executive coach who works with engineering founders: "the people I want to talk to by and large are too busy to be looking at LinkedIn, they're definitely not scrolling the feed." The franchise consultant has an agency doing LinkedIn outreach and said it feels like paying "to get likes for me on Facebook back in the day."

Brand marketing. The training firm has someone doing content and a new website. Their words: "less transactional and more of a kind of building the brand rather than actually bringing in new clients."

Buying leads. Lists of names a company sells to you, and often to your competitors too. The franchise consultant again: "it's not my leads." Twenty at a time, a few worth calling.

None of these are stupid. They share one flaw, though. Either you're interrupting someone who didn't ask, or you're waiting for someone to wander in.

Referrals worked because the buyer asked first.

## Paid traffic is the one with a dial

Paid traffic means paying Facebook, Instagram or Google to show an ad to people who look like your best clients, whether or not they've heard of you.

That's what my agency does. We run those ads for B2B firms and put meetings with buyers on their calendars. So, yes, I'm biased. Here's why I think it's the closest thing you can build to a referral on purpose.

Somebody with the problem sees your ad, recognizes their own situation in it, and asks to talk to you. They asked first. Same as a referral.

And unlike a referral, you can turn it up.

BUT!

Paid traffic done badly is just a more expensive way to get burned. Most firms I talk to that "tried ads" have a version of the same story. Monthly reports about how many people tapped the ad, and no new clients. Calls with people who never showed. An agency that ran everything in its own account and left with the data. A marketer who took the setup fee and stopped answering email.

So here's how to set it up so it actually replaces what the referrals were doing.

## How to replace referrals with paid traffic

**1. Put a number on the dry quarter.**
That training firm knows exactly what one quieter client cost: £150,000 a year. Work out yours. It tells you what a working channel is worth, and it makes the ad budget look a lot smaller.

**2. Write the ad from your best referral's words.**
Why did your last three referred clients call you? What did they say on that first call? Those sentences are your ad. Your best clients already did the copywriting.

**3. Send people to a conversation.**
The training firm's last agency sent the people who clicked their ads to a pre-recorded online course. Almost nobody watched past the free video. Referrals never worked that way. Nobody refers a friend to a course. They say, "Call this person." Paid traffic for a B2B service should end in a booked call.

**4. Ask a few questions before they can book.**
How big is the company, what's going on, how soon do they need help. It takes them a minute. The people who don't fit never get offered a time on your calendar, so you're not spending your afternoons on calls you'd never have taken.

**5. Own everything.**
The ad account is where your ads run from. It also keeps the record of everyone who ever saw or answered them, which is how the ads get better over time. One advisor told me they'd paid two different firms to run ads and never once had their own account. "I was just giving the ad agency the money." When they stopped, they got a spreadsheet of names. Everything the ads had learned stayed with the agency. Put the account in your company's name from day one.

**6. Pay for the thing you actually want.**
That leadership firm president again: "they think that they're going to tell me how many clicks we got last month and I'm going to be impressed... clicks doesn't matter." Write down up front what a good first call looks like for you (company size, budget, the problem they have) and only pay for those.

**7. Keep your referrals.**
None of this replaces word of mouth. It sits next to it. When the referrals are strong you've got extra. When a big client scales back, you've got something else running.

## "But my buyers aren't on Facebook"

I hear this a lot from B2B firms. The leadership firm president told me flat out that they'd "made the assumption it would never work" for C-suite buyers.

Your buyers are on LinkedIn during the day. Most of them are on Instagram and Facebook at night, like everyone else.

And their competitors know it. Meta, the company that owns Facebook and Instagram, keeps a public library of every ad running on its platforms. On one call I pulled it up for a well-known coaching program aimed at business owners. It had about 30 ads running at the same time.

They're not doing that for fun.

## What this looks like from your side of the desk

If you've never run an ad, here's what actually happens.

**First, we test.** Before real money goes in, we write a handful of short ads, each describing your buyer's problem a different way, mostly in the words your best clients used. We run them for a few days on a small budget. The ones YOUR buyers respond to tell us what YOUR market cares about. Only those get the real money.

Then picture the owner of a growing company, scrolling Instagram on a Sunday night.

**1. They see an ad that describes their week.**
Not "we offer leadership development solutions." Something closer to what your best client said on their first call with you. They stop, because it sounds like their own problem.

**2. They tap it and land on one short page about you.**
Not your homepage. Your homepage was built for people who already know you, like a referral looking you up before the first call. A stranger needs something simpler: who you help, what the first conversation is, and what happens on it.

**3. They answer a few quick questions.**
How big their company is, what's going on, how soon they need help. Anyone who isn't a fit never gets offered a time, so your calendar only fills with people you'd actually want to talk to.

**4. If they fit, they pick a time on your calendar right there.**
No back-and-forth over email.

**5. They get reminders before the call.**
By email and text, automatically. People who booked a stranger off an ad forget. Reminders are a big part of the difference between a booked call and a held one.

**6. You get on a call with someone who already raised their hand.**
They know what you do, they told you what's going on, and they picked the time. That's about as close as a stranger gets to a referral.

Two more things, because they're where firms usually get burned:

**You own all of it.** The ad account is in your company's name and you pay Facebook directly on your own card. If we ever part ways, everything stays with you.

**You pay us per qualified call.** Before we start, you and I write down what "qualified" means for your firm. You pay us when someone who meets that bar gets on a call with you. Not for clicks, not for names, and not for no-shows.
