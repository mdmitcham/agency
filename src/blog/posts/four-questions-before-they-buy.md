Somebody has to say yes to you four times before they say yes to you once.

Not four times out loud. Four times in their head, quietly, in an order they don't know they're following.

I saw Brennan Hopkins make this point in a post a couple weeks back and it's been rattling around since, mostly because it explains a thing I've watched go wrong for years without having a clean name for it.

Here are the four.

- **Do I actually have this problem?**
- **Does anything actually fix it?**
- **Why would I believe you, specifically?**
- **What happens if I say yes right now?**

Simple enough that it feels obvious.

Then you go read your own cold email and realize it's trying to answer all four in six sentences to somebody who has never heard your name.

## The order is not optional

You can't answer question three before question one.

This is the part that gets skipped. A stranger who has not yet agreed they have a problem does not care about your case study. They don't care that you worked with a clinic in Chicago. They don't care how many leads you generated last quarter.

Credentials are the answer to a question they haven't asked yet.

Gene Schwartz put the constraint about as plainly as anyone ever has:

> "Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already existing desires onto a particular product."

You don't get to install question one. You can only find people who already have it and say it back to them better than they can say it themselves.

Everything after that is sequencing.

## Four questions, four assets

Here's where I think most outbound falls apart.

Each question wants a different asset. Different length, different job, different moment. And we keep trying to make one thing carry all four.

The email says: here is a problem, and I can prove I understand your business.

The owner says: fine, but does anything actually work?

The email says: WE ALSO HAVE A 4.9 STAR RATING.

Nobody's talking to each other.

So split them up.

Question one is the email's whole job. Name the problem specifically enough that they feel seen. That's the entire assignment.

Question two is a demonstration. Show the mechanism. Not "we run ads," but the actual move, and the reason it works when the last three agencies didn't.

Question three is proof, and it's the expensive one. Video testimonials. Numbers in writing. A real page on a real domain. Something a skeptical person can poke at without having to talk to you first.

Question four is the call, where you say what it costs and what happens Monday.

The email gets one job because six sentences is one job's worth of room.

## Why question three is the hard one

Because you're a stranger.

There's an old framework from the launch world. Agree to the problem, then agree to the solution, then agree to the product. Three agreements, and it works beautifully.

For warm audiences.

BUT!

It quietly assumes the reader already knows who you are. Somebody who's been on a list for eight months has been answering question three passively the whole time, one email at a time, without ever noticing they were doing it.

Cold outreach doesn't get that runway.

You show up in an inbox with no history and no reason for anyone to give you the benefit of the doubt. So question three stops being a step in your sequence and becomes the tax you pay on being unknown. You pay it before anything else you built gets to matter.

Which is why the proof asset keeps getting postponed and keeps being the thing that was missing.

## What's going on now

We split the questions across the pipeline on purpose.

The email does question one only. One problem, named specifically, in language the owner would actually use. No credentials, no logo soup, no paragraph about our process. If it's doing anything other than making somebody think "yeah, that's my week," it's too long.

The deck does two and three. It shows the specific mechanism and it shows the receipts, and it does it asynchronously so nobody has to sit through a discovery call to find out whether we're real.

The call does four. Three numbers, in writing, before anybody asks. What it costs, what it's expected to return, what happens if it doesn't.

We're also building the thing that should have existed a year ago: one page, real domain, pricing in writing, testimonial links you can send in thirty seconds. Question three, sitting there permanently, instead of getting reassembled by hand every time somebody hesitates.

Most of the outreach I see is a question-three asset stapled to a question-four ask, mailed to somebody who hasn't answered question one.

Then everyone concludes cold email is dead. 🤷
