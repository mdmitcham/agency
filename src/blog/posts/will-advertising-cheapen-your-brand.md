"I don't want to be found on Facebook from a business perspective. That's not consistent with our brand."

That's a strategy consultant whose clients run some of the largest companies in the country. Thirty years in the business. It's a fair worry.

You've spent years building a reputation as the serious option. You don't want to show up between a meme and a mattress sale looking like everyone else who's yelling for attention.

An executive coach I talked to felt the same pull, then argued themselves out of it on the call:

"You don't want to trash your brand in the marketplace, but at the same time... a shot on goal is never a bad play."

And then the part that settled it for them:

"None of those people are customers right now. And if they... decide that they're not going to be a customer, we haven't lost anything."

## The channel doesn't cheapen you. The ad does.

Think about what actually makes an ad feel cheap.

Fake urgency. "Only 3 spots left!" Stock photos of people in suits shaking hands. Promises nobody believes. Shouting.

None of that comes from Facebook. It comes from the person who wrote the ad.

A thoughtful ad from a serious firm looks like what it is. Somebody who understands a hard problem, describing it better than the reader has ever heard it described.

David Ogilvy put it in print in 1963, and it still holds:

> The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife.
>
> <footer>— David Ogilvy, <em>Confessions of an Advertising Man</em></footer>

Your buyer is smart. Write to them like they are and the ad does your reputation a favor.

## What a premium ad sounds like

We run ads for a retirement planning firm. When we tested what their prospects responded to, the ads that won weren't the loudest ones. They were the ones that sounded like a client talking.

Statements written as a private admission, like "I spent 35 years counting on a paycheck to show up, and now pulling my income from my retirement portfolio feels uncomfortable in a way I didn't expect," did well.

When we rewrote similar ideas as a pitch aimed at the reader, they mostly did worse. The most accusatory version did worst of all.

Confession beat accusation.

That's good news if you care about your brand. The ad that respects the reader is often the ad that works.

## The bigger brand risk is being invisible

That strategy consultant made another point on our call that stuck with me. So much of what's on LinkedIn now is written by AI that "the value of it... is declining."

Every firm is posting. Most of it sounds the same.

Meanwhile, a firm nobody has heard of doesn't have a brand problem. It has an awareness problem. Your reputation only helps you with the people who've heard of you.
